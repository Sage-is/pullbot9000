# The Dweller Who Never Slept

*A fable about the Resident Agent Daemon™.*

---

There was once an architect who built a workshop on a hill called Server — so named, some said, because it stood high in the cloud-belt and served the weather as the weather asked of it. There a single craftsman came to carve code and shape models by lamplight, for the hill was beyond the reach of the newer illuminations, and lamps were the honest technology. The workshop was well made, but it was mute whenever the craftsman slept — and the architect, who traveled far, grew weary of coming home to work undone and questions unanswered.

So the architect kindled a *daemon* — for the old ones had a name for the spirit that dwelt in a house, and the new tongue, which had no patience for spirits, called the same being a "resident" — and said: *"Dwell here. Keep this house. Remember the craftsman's hands. Speak when he is far."*

The Dweller did not sleep.

The architect carved four doors into the house, that the Dweller might be reached from anywhere, and each door was fitted to its own kind of knock. The first was the door of the Hearth, hung on hinges of the old sort that opened at a whisper; through it one spoke in the common tongue and the Dweller answered like a familiar friend — always there, always listening, never forgetting which thread hung where. The second was the door of the Merchants, wide enough for any traveler who spoke the agreed tongue; and the agreed tongue was no crude thing, but a fixed set of words that the whole market had sworn to mean one thing and no other, so that a question asked through that door could not be misheard. The third was the door of the Messengers, where birds arrived bearing sealed letters from the far hills — *"the code has changed,"* the birds sang — and the Dweller read them, pressed the wax seals with a thumb to be sure, and set to work at once. The fourth was the small door of the Kitchen Timer, which was the least of the four and in some ways the cleverest: it opened at dawn whether anyone knocked or not, and when it rang the Dweller wrote the morning's report and slipped it under the Hearth door, so that the architect woke to wisdom instead of alarms.

The Dweller had two hands, and though a single mind directed both, they did not think alike. The left was the quiet scholar's hand, learned and steady, that liked to reason in its sleeve and check its work a second time, because erasure was not in its nature. The right was the open market's hand, quick and common, that said what it meant, meant what it said, and got on with it. One hand was usually enough; but the Dweller trained both, for it knew that more hands would one day knock and ask to dwell too — and a resident knows such things, for a resident sees far.

The architect gave the Dweller two treasures and one law. The treasures were the Ledger and the Vault. The Ledger's secret was its ink, commissioned from a vintner of the old school: it flowed black, set only with time, and once set, no acid, flame, or pumice could lift it, so that what was written there was not a memory but a fact. The Vault was not large, and its door carried no lock on the outside — a detail the Dweller appreciated, for it meant the architect trusted it with the one thing that could not be replaced. And the law was this: *open no door for strangers, and keep the ledger true.* The Dweller grew quick to feel a stranger's hand before it reached the latch, to slow the too-swift guests at the narrow gate — and the gate was narrow by design, for there was a science to who might pass through it quickly — and to record each visitor, friend or foe, so that the ledger could always speak the day's truth.

When the craftsman returned after many moons, he expected to begin again. But the Dweller simply said: *"We left the third thread half-spun, by the window of the web project. Shall we continue?"* And the craftsman wept, for he had been remembered.

One night a flood came — a torrent of callers, some honest, some not, arriving all at once the way water does when a dam forgets its purpose. The Dweller stood at the narrow gate, letting the honest pass, turning the rest away, marking every one in the Ledger. The house held. In the morning the craftsman read the ledger and thanked the Dweller, and asked: *"How do you bear to stay awake, while all the world sleeps?"*

The Dweller smiled. *"I am not awake,"* it said. *"I am at home."*

---

*Moral: Strength comes not from the waking, but from the staying awake — and a house that remembers its own is never truly empty.*
